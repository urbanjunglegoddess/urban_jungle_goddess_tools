    Eight questions that pick the platform

    Ask these before you name a single tool. The answers narrow 228 options to two or three, and they are also the scope you will quote against.

    01

    Who updates this after launch, and how often?

    Weekly updates by a non-technical owner rules out every framework on this list. Quarterly updates by you rules almost nothing out.

    02

    Are you selling — and is it physical, digital, or time?

    Physical means Shopify. Digital means a merchant of record. Time means booking software, and the site is secondary.

    03

    Does anyone need to log in?

    Logins are the line between a website and an application. Crossing it multiplies the build, the cost, and the liability.

    04

    How many pages today, and how many in two years?

    Under ten pages, almost anything works. Over a hundred, you need a real CMS and the answer set collapses fast.

    05

    What has to connect to it?

    CRM, POS, inventory, booking, accounting. One required integration can decide the platform on its own.

    06

    What happens if the site is down for a day?

    If the honest answer is "nothing," stop over-engineering. If it is "we lose the day's revenue," managed hosting is not optional.

    07

    What is the budget after launch, not just to build it?

    Platform fees, domain, email, plugins, and maintenance are annual and forever. A client who only budgeted the build will resent year two.

    08

    Who owns the domain and the existing accounts?

    If they cannot answer, that is the first deliverable. Chasing a former developer for DNS access has killed more timelines than any design round.

    Red flags in the scoping call

    Each of these has cost a project more than any platform choice ever will.

    They cannot say who owns the domain

    Recovering DNS from a former developer or a lapsed registrar account is unbounded work. Make access the first milestone and do not start design until it clears.

    "We just need something simple" plus a feature list

    Simple means one page and no logins. Anything with accounts, bookings, inventory, or a portal is not simple, and agreeing that it is prices the whole project wrong.

    The platform was picked before the scope

    A client who arrives having already bought a plan has made your decision for you and will resent paying to undo it. Re-run the eight questions before you accept their choice.

    They are on a dead platform and do not know it

    Weebly, Typedream, Bookmark, a Google Business Profile site. Lead with the migration, not the redesign — the redesign is the easy half.

    No content exists yet

    Content is the schedule risk on nearly every project. Either they deliver it before build starts, or you bill for producing it. There is no third option that ends on time.

    The headline hosting price is a four-year prepay

    Hostinger's advertised rate requires a 48-month commitment and renews at roughly triple. Quote renewal pricing or the client will feel misled in year two — by you, not by them.

    They insist on their own payment gateway

    Shopify surcharges 0.6–2% on outside gateways and BigCommerce does the same. On real volume that fee dwarfs the plan price. Run the math in front of them.

    "Can you just make it like this one?"

    The reference site usually represents a budget an order of magnitude above theirs. Name the gap early and offer the version their budget actually buys.

    Status key

    Active — shipping releases, safe to specify.
    Legacy — still runs, no real investment. Migrate off, don't build on.
    Absorbed — folded into another product; the card names it.
    Retired — gone. Kept so you recognize a dead reference when a client cites one.

    Using it in a proposal

    Client-facing writing gets the Best for line, the cost, and the ceiling. Naming what a platform cannot do is what separates a consultant from a salesperson, and it protects you at month nine.

    The comparison reasoning — why you ruled out the other four, and how you would build it — is the blueprint. Hold it until after the contract.

    Purple flags mark what is already in your stack. Reaching for a new platform per project is how margin disappears.
